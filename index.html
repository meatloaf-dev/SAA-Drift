About this atlas

Click anywhere on the map to probe the field at that point. Use the slider or ▶ to scrub through 130 years of geomagnetic change — watch the anomaly deepen and march west, and the north magnetic pole race from Arctic Canada toward Siberia. The final four years (2027–2030) are a published forecast, not a measurement — shown with a dashed badge and boundary.

Core field model, 1997–2026 ESA Swarm MCO_SHA_2X product (CHAOS‑Core, DTU Space) — fit to real Swarm, CryoSat‑2, Ørsted and ground‑observatory magnetic measurements. Queried from ESA's vires.services API on a recurring schedule, so this stays current without a manual rebuild.
Core field model, 1900–1995 IGRF‑14 (IAGA / NOAA NCEI), the public reference model, evaluated at the same 460 km altitude for historical continuity before Swarm's launch — also via vires.services.
Forecast, 2027–2030 IGRF‑14's predictive secular‑variation segment — the same kind of 5‑year-ahead forecast NOAA/BGS publish as the World Magnetic Model. A projection of rate, shape and trajectory, not an observation.
Magnetic north pole track NOAA NCEI historical & predicted dip‑pole positions, 1590–2025 (ngdc.noaa.gov/geomag/data/poles).

Reading the three magnetic layers

The globe can draw three different things, and they are easy to confuse because all three are "magnetic field lines." They measure different quantities and they run in different directions.

Field intensity (F)

Isodynamic lines — contours of total field strength, in nanotesla. This is the layer that shows the South Atlantic Anomaly: a broad region where the field is roughly a third weaker than elsewhere at the same latitude. Measured at 460 km, satellite altitude, because that is where the weakening actually matters — it is why spacecraft power down instruments crossing it.

Declination (D) — runs north–south

Isogonic lines — contours of the angle between magnetic north and true north. The agonic line is the zero contour, where a compass happens to point at true north. Because it tracks a bearing, it runs roughly north–south, pole to pole.

Inclination (I) — runs east–west

Isoclinic lines — contours of magnetic dip, the angle the field makes with the horizontal. Straight down at the north magnetic pole (+90°), straight up at the south (−90°), and perfectly level along the dip equator (0°). These are the lines that genuinely run east–west, and the dip equator is the one that has been migrating north. Switch on ◇ Isoclinic lines to draw this family over either of the other layers, so isogonic and isoclinic can be read together the way a paper magnetic chart shows them — the agonic line and the dip equator cross over northern South America.

Overlays

Volcanoes

Every Holocene volcano in the Smithsonian Institution's Global Volcanism Program catalogue, with those that have erupted since 1900 picked out brighter. Refreshed on the same weekly schedule as the field data, so eruption years stay current.

CDC mortality

Age-adjusted death rates by state from CDC/NCHS, on the declination layer, for heart disease, cancer, diabetes and Alzheimer's. The year follows the epoch slider across the 1999–2017 span the state data covers, so the map moves through time with the field. Rates are deaths per 100,000 standardised to the 2000 US population — mortality, not prevalence — and the colour scale is fixed across all years, so the steep national decline in heart disease is visible as you scrub rather than being normalised away.

Outside 1999–2017 nothing is drawn, and the shaded span on the epoch slider shows where the data actually is. Painting the nearest year instead would fill the map for epochs with nothing behind them.

Why that span and no wider: CDC stopped maintaining this series — it was last updated in October 2019 and ends at 2017. Earlier years do not exist in CDC's open data at all; state-level cause-of-death before 1999 survives only in the archival Compressed Mortality File, split across two ICD revisions that changed how causes are coded. Later years exist only as provisional weekly counts with no age breakdown, which cannot be age-standardised, so putting them on this scale would make a state's rank shift with its age structure rather than its health.

For heart disease and cancer a national series does reach back to 1900, so outside the mapped span the legend gives that figure instead — which is how you can watch heart-disease mortality climb to its 1950 peak of 589 per 100,000 and fall to 164 by 2018. Diabetes and Alzheimer's have no comparable long series. Dementia is not coded separately from Alzheimer's in this file.

UVB latitudes

Where the midday sun climbs above ~45° elevation, the threshold usually cited for vitamin D synthesis in skin. This is pure solar geometry — latitude and the 23.44° tilt of Earth's axis, nothing else. It takes no account of cloud, ozone, altitude or aerosol.

Where the agonic line actually runs

The source's map draws the agonic line as a fixed polyline of eleven points. Measured against the computed line for the current epoch, it sits 8.7° to 16.6° east of where zero declination now falls, averaging 13.6°. Fitted against every epoch in this atlas it matches the line as it stood around 1940 to within 2°, and the match degrades steadily after that: the agonic line has drifted west across the Americas at roughly 0.11–0.14° a year for decades. Switch the epoch slider back to 1940 and the computed line settles onto that polyline.

Eighty-six years of drift is too much to carry into a ranking that scores distance from the line, so nothing here is modelled on that polyline. The agonic line this atlas draws is computed from the live field at whatever epoch the slider is on, and the Dec scores below are recomputed against it.

Ranked sites — tiers 1 and 2

Tiers, names and the five published scores are from the source. Mag and Dec are the two axes it defines with explicit numeric thresholds — |dec| under 2° scoring 5, surface intensity above 55,000 nT scoring 5, and so on — so both are recomputed here from the live field rather than carried over. Nine of the seventeen sites change. UVB, Vol and SAA have no published thresholds and pass through unchanged, each with the measured quantity beside it: months a year the midday sun clears 45°, kilometres to the nearest volcano that has erupted since 1900, and kilometres to the current anomaly centre.

Recomputing moves the Central American sites in both directions. El Salvador and Guatemala hold Dec 5 — both sit 0.4° off true zero declination, closer to the real line than the source's own map puts them. Costa Rica and Nicaragua drop to Dec 4 at 2.1° and 2.3°, just outside the 2° band. Taiwan and Sicily gain a Mag point, Iceland and Kamchatka lose one. Everything recomputes on each weekly refresh, so these track the field as it moves.

Switch on ◆ Tier 1–2 sites on the declination layer to see them against the line.

Source

These layers follow the factors set out in “Where to Live — Magnetic Inclination × Declination × UVB × nnEMF” (v8, May 2026), compiled by jespada drawing on the work of Jack Kruse. That document identifies five factors — field intensity, declination relative to the agonic line, magnetic inclination, active volcanism, and UVB availability by latitude. All five are available on this globe, together with distance to the anomaly centre in the probe readout.

Each is drawn here from its primary source rather than transcribed. The magnetic layers are computed from ESA's Swarm mission and the CHAOS and IGRF‑14 models through the VirES API — the same models that document cites, including Finlay, Kloss & Gillet (2025) and NOAA's World Magnetic Model. Volcanism comes from the Smithsonian catalogue, and the UVB bounds from solar geometry.

Values are recomputed from the live models on a weekly schedule, so they track the current epoch rather than a fixed snapshot, and the epoch slider runs them back to 1900 and forward through the IGRF‑14 forecast to 2030.
